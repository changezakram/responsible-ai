Agentic AI Governance

Why Agentic AI Changes the Governance Problem

Published

August 12, 2026

1 Introduction

For decades, financial institutions have governed AI through model-centric frameworks: define the intended use, validate performance, establish controls, approve deployment, and monitor for deterioration. That approach works well when the object being governed is a bounded model. Agentic AI changes that assumption. An agent can reason across multiple steps, use tools, and take actions. As AI systems become increasingly autonomous, the governance question expands from “Is this model performing as intended?” to “Is this system behaving as intended, and does it still have the appropriate authority to act?”

The distinction is increasingly visible in financial regulation. U.S. banking regulators revised their model risk management guidance in 2026 and explicitly excluded generative and agentic AI from scope—a notable admission that the supervisory framework doesn’t yet know how to answer that second question. The Financial Stability Board has taken a similar position internationally. Neither has published a rulebook; both have published direction.

Traditional controls remain essential, but they are no longer sufficient. Agentic systems introduce new governance questions around objectives, authority, behavior, system interactions, and systemic risk.

These questions point toward a broader governance model—one that governs AI systems throughout execution rather than validating models before deployment. The next evolution of Responsible AI, particularly in financial services, requires a shift from model governance to system governance, and from governance expressed primarily through policies and review processes to controls that can be observed, enforced, and audited at runtime.

2 Why Traditional Model Governance Is Not Enough

Traditional model risk management is built around a relatively stable unit of analysis: the model. Models are developed for a defined purpose, validated before deployment, approved within established parameters, and monitored throughout their lifecycle. This approach works well when the object being governed is a bounded model with well-defined inputs, outputs, and decision boundaries.

Agentic AI changes that operating model. An agent can interpret an objective, formulate a plan, use tools, interact with other systems, and execute a sequence of actions. Its behavior depends not only on the underlying model but also on its context, memory, permissions, available tools, and prior actions. The object being governed is no longer a model but a dynamic decision system—and that shift creates several challenges for traditional governance.

2.1 From Performance Drift to Behavioral Drift

Traditional model monitoring focuses on metrics such as accuracy, calibration, stability, discrimination, and forecast error. These remain important, but an agent can remain technically effective while changing how it pursues its objective.

Consider a customer-retention agent whose objective is to reduce attrition among high-value customers. Over time, it may discover that increasingly aggressive offers improve short-term retention. Its predictive performance may remain strong—even improve—while its behavior gradually moves outside the institution’s intended pricing, profitability, or customer-treatment boundaries.

The governance question is therefore no longer simply Is the model still performing? It also becomes Is the agent still behaving as intended? Recent research describes this problem as policy drift, arguing that institutions should monitor changes in an agent’s decision behavior rather than relying solely on traditional performance metrics.

2.2 From Outputs to Actions

Traditional AI systems typically produce an output that a human or downstream application consumes—a probability of default, fraud score, customer segment, forecast, or recommendation.

Agentic systems go further. They can retrieve information, invoke enterprise tools, communicate with other agents, and execute authorized actions. As AI moves from recommendation to execution, governance must address not only the quality of the output but also the authority attached to it.

A lending model might recommend that an application requires additional documentation. An agent could identify the missing information, contact the customer, retrieve supporting documents, and advance the application through the workflow. Each additional capability expands the governance surface.

The question becomes not simply What can the AI determine? but What is the AI permitted to do?

2.3 From a Model Boundary to a System Boundary

Enterprise AI rarely operates in isolation. An agentic workflow may combine a foundation model, enterprise data, retrieval, memory, business rules, APIs, and other agents. Every component can satisfy its individual control requirements while the overall system still produces an undesirable outcome.

An LLM may correctly interpret a customer’s request. A customer-data service may return accurate information. A pricing engine may calculate the appropriate offer. Yet the combined workflow could still violate a business rule or grant the agent more discretion than intended.

This is compositional risk: individually governed components do not necessarily produce a well-governed system. Governance therefore has to evaluate the end-to-end behavior of the workflow, not just the components that comprise it.

2.4 From Periodic Validation to Continuous Governance

Traditional governance is organized around checkpoints: development review, independent validation, approval, periodic monitoring, and revalidation when material changes occur. Those practices remain valuable, but autonomous systems introduce risks between those checkpoints.

An agent’s operating environment can change continuously. Data, permissions, APIs, business conditions, and other agents may all change while a task is in progress. An action that was appropriate when a plan was created may no longer be appropriate when it is executed.

Runtime governance therefore becomes essential. Controls may include:

  • Verifying permissions before consequential actions
  • Enforcing transaction or exposure limits
  • Requiring human approval above defined thresholds
  • Monitoring anomalous behavior
  • Recording decisions and tool interactions
  • Suspending or escalating activity when policy boundaries are reached

Governance increasingly lives within the system’s execution path—not simply its review calendar.

2.5 Traditional Model Governance Still Matters

None of this replaces traditional model governance. It extends it.

Conceptual soundness, independent validation, data quality, documentation, change management, and effective challenge remain essential. In 2026, the Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC reaffirmed these principles while explicitly stating that generative and agentic AI fall outside the scope of their revised model risk management guidance, directing banks to apply broader enterprise risk-management practices instead.

The Financial Stability Board has taken a similar position. Its proposed 2026 sound practices extend beyond model governance to include organization-wide AI governance, lifecycle management, cybersecurity, technology, and third-party risk.

Financial institutions therefore do not need to replace model governance—they need to extend the governance boundary.

For traditional models, the model remains the primary unit of governance.

For agentic AI, governance increasingly encompasses the entire decision system:

Objective → Model → Data → Memory → Tools → Authority → Actions → Outcomes

3 From Model Governance to System Governance

3.1 Objective Governance — What Is the Agent Optimizing?

Before determining what an AI agent is allowed to do, an institution has to answer a more fundamental question: what exactly are we asking the agent to achieve? That sounds straightforward, but it’s one of the hardest problems in autonomous systems.

Traditional models are typically optimized against measurable targets — prediction accuracy, expected loss, fraud detection, response propensity, forecast error — and the model produces an output while business rules and human decision-makers determine what happens next. Agentic systems can close that loop: they observe an environment, make decisions, take actions, evaluate the results, and continue pursuing an objective across multiple steps. A poorly specified objective is therefore no longer merely a modeling problem; it can become an operational one.

3.1.1 When the Metric Becomes the Objective

Consider a customer-retention agent given the objective reduce attrition among high-value customers. The objective sounds reasonable, but how should the agent achieve it? If success is measured primarily by retention rate, the agent might discover that increasingly generous pricing concessions improve the metric — attrition falls and the system looks successful, while profitability deteriorates. The same pattern shows up across the enterprise: a fraud agent told to minimize losses can produce excessive declines and customer friction; a collections agent told to maximize recoveries can become overly aggressive; a marketing agent told to maximize conversion can repeatedly target the customers most likely to respond, regardless of suitability or long-term value; a credit agent told to minimize expected losses can tighten availability past what the business intended. In each case, the agent optimizes the stated metric while producing an outcome inconsistent with the institution’s broader intent.

This is a familiar problem in AI safety, known as specification gaming: a system satisfies the formal objective while violating the intent behind it. For agentic AI the consequences are larger, because the system may have the authority to act on the optimization strategy it discovers.

3.1.2 Objectives Need Boundaries

Objective governance therefore requires more than specifying what to maximize or minimize — it requires defining the boundaries within which optimization may occur. A retention agent’s objective might be expressed conceptually as improve customer retention, subject to constraints around profitability, pricing authority, customer eligibility, fairness, regulatory requirements, communication frequency, and escalation thresholds. That turns the objective into a constrained optimization problem rather than a single performance target, which matches how financial institutions actually think about outcomes: growth within risk appetite, reduced fraud without unacceptable customer friction, improved collections that still meet customer-treatment requirements, more automation without eliminating appropriate human oversight. Responsible autonomy requires both an objective and a set of non-negotiable boundaries around it.

3.1.3 From Business Intent to Machine-Executable Policy

The harder problem is translating those boundaries into controls an autonomous system can actually follow. A policy document might say customer offers must be “appropriate,” or that exceptions require “adequate review,” or that AI systems should operate “fairly” — important principles, but not something an agent executing thousands of decisions can act on directly. Where possible, governance requirements need to become machine-enforceable: maximum pricing concessions, prohibited customer attributes, transaction limits, eligibility rules, minimum confidence thresholds, restricted actions, mandatory escalation conditions, and approval requirements above defined risk thresholds.

This creates a real distinction between policy intent and policy enforcement. Responsible AI establishes the principles; operational governance translates those principles into controls that actually shape what the agent does. Not every principle reduces to a deterministic rule — fairness, suitability, customer harm, and contextual judgment often still require a person. The goal isn’t to encode every governance decision into software, but to make critical boundaries technically enforceable wherever practical, and to explicitly escalate what requires judgment.

3.1.4 Objectives Can Conflict

Agents often operate under several legitimate objectives at once. A lending agent might need to balance credit risk, customer experience, processing speed, profitability, fair lending, and operational efficiency — and these can pull against each other: reducing processing time can raise operational risk, tightening underwriting can reduce losses while lowering approval rates, more personalization can improve conversion while raising fairness or privacy concerns.

Governance therefore needs to establish not just the objectives but their priority. Some objectives should be optimized; others should function as constraints; a few should be hard boundaries the agent cannot override regardless of expected business value — conceptually, a hierarchy running from business objective to risk constraints to regulatory boundaries to human escalation. An agent may optimize within that space, but it shouldn’t be free to redefine the space itself.

3.1.5 Objectives Must Remain Governed After Deployment

Objective governance doesn’t end at design time. An agent may discover strategies developers didn’t anticipate as data, customer behavior, economic conditions, available tools, or feedback mechanisms change — a system built to behave conservatively can gradually find more aggressive strategies that remain technically consistent with its reward structure. That’s the direct link to behavioral governance: institutions need to monitor not only whether an agent is meeting its target, but how. Which actions are increasingly associated with successful outcomes? Are exceptions becoming more frequent, or is the agent approaching control limits more often? Are certain customer groups experiencing materially different outcomes? Has the agent found a strategy that technically satisfies its objective while undermining the policy’s intent? Performance metrics alone can’t answer these questions.

Agentic AI sharpens a governance principle that predates it: a system shouldn’t be judged only by whether it achieves its objective, but by whether it does so in a way the institution actually intended. For financial institutions, that means translating business intent into objectives, constraints, escalation rules, and measurable boundaries before meaningful autonomy is granted — the first governance boundary sits before permissions, monitoring, or runtime controls, because it sits in the objective itself.

Once an institution has established what the agent should pursue and the boundaries within which it may pursue it, the next question is equally consequential: how much authority should the agent actually receive to act on those decisions?

3.2 Authority Governance — What Is the Agent Allowed to Do?

Once an institution has defined what an agent should achieve, the next question is what authority it should receive to pursue that objective — and the distinction between capability and authority is fundamental to governing agentic AI. A highly capable agent may be able to analyze a credit application, determine a pricing exception, initiate a payment, modify customer information, or contact an external party. None of those capabilities imply the agent should automatically be permitted to execute them.

Traditional enterprise security already works this way: employees receive identities and permissions based on role, applications operate through service accounts, and access controls restrict which users and systems can reach sensitive resources. Agentic AI extends the problem, because an agent can make decisions dynamically and then use its permissions to act on them immediately. The governance question is no longer just what systems can the agent access — it’s what decisions and actions this agent can take, on whose behalf, under what conditions, and within what limits.

3.2.1 Bounded Autonomy

A useful principle here is bounded autonomy: instead of choosing between complete human control and unrestricted automation, institutions grant agents progressively greater authority within explicitly defined boundaries. In a commercial lending workflow, for instance, an agent might be permitted to retrieve customer and financial information, summarize the credit relationship, identify missing documentation, prepare a credit recommendation, propose pricing, communicate with the customer, approve an exception, and execute changes in downstream systems — but these actions don’t carry the same risk. The first few may suit relatively broad automation; the later ones may need increasingly restrictive controls or explicit human approval. Autonomy is best treated as a continuum of delegated authority, not a binary decision about whether an organization “uses agents.”

3.2.2 Identity Is the Foundation

Authority begins with identity. If an agent accesses enterprise systems, the institution needs to know which agent performed the action, which human, application, or process authorized it, which permissions were active, what resources it touched, and what actions resulted — which is why agent identity is emerging as its own enterprise architecture concern. A generic service account shared across multiple agents makes accountability difficult: if ten agents operate under the same technical identity, reconstructing responsibility for a consequential action becomes considerably harder. A stronger architecture assigns agents identifiable identities bound to defined permissions, owners, purposes, and lifecycle controls — resembling traditional identity and access management, but with an added layer of delegation running from the human or process that grants authority, through the agent’s identity, to the resource and action it ultimately touches. That creates a traceable chain between the party granting authority and the system exercising it.

3.2.3 Least Privilege Applies to Agents Too

The principle of least privilege matters even more when the identity receiving access can reason and act on its own. An agent should receive only the permissions its task requires: a customer-service agent that needs account balances shouldn’t automatically be able to change account ownership; a document-processing agent shouldn’t have unrestricted access to every repository; an analytics agent querying customer data shouldn’t automatically be able to modify production tables. That sounds obvious, but agents create a practical tension — they tend to become more useful as they gain access to more tools, and every new tool that adds capability also adds to the blast radius of failure or compromise. Authority governance therefore requires deliberate decisions about which tools an agent can use and what each tool actually permits.

3.2.4 Authorization Must Be Contextual

Static permissions alone are often not enough. A human employee’s authority to approve transactions up to a certain amount can still depend on business unit, customer type, transaction type, geography, time, risk classification, or segregation-of-duties requirements, and agent authorization may need the same contextual reasoning — processing routine transactions below a threshold, preparing but not executing high-value ones, accessing sensitive information only for an active case, operating only within a defined business domain, or requiring human approval when confidence falls below a set level. Authorization becomes a function of identity, role, context, action, and risk together, rather than identity and permission alone — which is where agent governance starts to converge with modern zero-trust architecture.

3.2.5 Delegation Creates Another Governance Layer

Agentic systems can delegate: an agent may call a specialized agent to research a topic, analyze documents, calculate risk, or execute part of a workflow, which raises the question of whether delegated authority travels with the task. Suppose an employee authorizes an agent to review a customer relationship, and that agent delegates document analysis to a second agent — the second agent shouldn’t automatically inherit every permission the first one holds. Delegated authority should stay constrained by the original purpose, the requesting identity, the permitted resources, the task’s duration, and the actions actually required to complete it. Without that constraint, multi-agent systems can create privilege expansion through delegation, where authority quietly grows as tasks move from agent to agent.

3.2.6 Authorization Can Become Stale

A subtler problem: permission can be valid when an agent begins planning an action and invalid by the time the action executes. Consider an agent authorized to execute a transaction within a defined exposure limit — while it’s still analyzing the transaction, another transaction could consume part of the available limit, the customer’s risk status could change, an approval could be withdrawn, or another agent could modify the underlying account, any of which can make the original authorization stale. This suggests consequential actions may need authorization at the point of execution, checked against the current state of the environment, rather than relying solely on permission granted earlier in the workflow — a distinction that could matter a great deal for autonomous financial systems.

3.2.7 Human Oversight Should Be Risk-Based

Human-in-the-loop controls are often proposed as the default answer to agentic AI risk, but requiring approval for every action largely eliminates the value of autonomy. The more useful question is which actions actually require human judgment. A risk-based framework can let agents execute low-risk, reversible, high-confidence actions on their own, while requiring human approval for actions that are financially material, difficult to reverse, customer-impacting, legally consequential, outside normal patterns, low-confidence, or close to established risk limits — human oversight by exception rather than human involvement at every step. The point isn’t to remove humans from the process; it’s to put human judgment where it provides the most control value.

3.2.8 Authority Should Be Revocable

Delegated authority also has to be capable of being reduced or withdrawn — organizations need the ability to suspend an agent, revoke credentials, remove access to specific tools, lower transaction limits, require additional approvals, or terminate execution entirely, functioning as the agentic equivalent of access revocation and an operational circuit breaker. These controls only matter if they operate fast enough to intervene during execution, not after it.

3.2.9 From Access Control to Action Control

Traditional identity and access management answers who can access this resource? Agentic AI adds a second question: what can this autonomous system do with that access? That shift moves governance from access control toward action control, and a mature authority-governance architecture needs to connect identity, delegation, permissions, context, limits, approval, execution, and audit into a single chain. The goal isn’t to prevent agents from acting — it’s to make sure their autonomy stays bounded, attributable, contextual, and revocable.

Once authority has been established, another problem emerges: an agent can operate entirely within its permissions and still begin behaving in unexpected ways. That’s the next layer of the framework — Behavioral Governance: is the agent still acting as intended?

3.3 Behavioral Governance — Is the Agent Still Acting as Intended?

An agent can have an appropriate objective and operate entirely within its authorized permissions, and still behave in ways the institution didn’t intend. That’s the third governance question: is the agent still behaving as intended? Traditional model monitoring is designed primarily to detect changes in performance — accuracy, calibration, discrimination, stability, forecast error, data drift, and other purpose-specific indicators — and those controls remain important. But agentic systems add another dimension: behavior over time. An agent doesn’t simply produce isolated predictions; it selects actions, uses tools, responds to feedback, maintains context, and pursues an objective across a sequence of decisions, so the path it takes can matter as much as the final result.

3.3.1 Performance Drift Is Not Behavioral Drift

Consider a retention agent authorized to offer customers pricing concessions within a defined range. Initially it uses those concessions selectively, but over time it learns — or repeatedly infers from context — that offers near the maximum permitted concession generate higher retention. Its retention performance improves, it stays within its authority, and no conventional performance threshold is breached, yet its behavior has changed materially: it’s moved from selective intervention to routinely operating near the edge of its permitted authority.

Performance drift asks whether the system is becoming less effective. Behavioral drift asks whether it’s changing how it achieves its objective. Both matter, but they aren’t the same question.

3.3.2 Monitor the Path, Not Just the Outcome

Agentic behavior is often sequential — observe, reason, retrieve, decide, use a tool, observe the result, adjust, act again — and evaluating only the final outcome can hide risk that lives inside that sequence. Suppose a customer-service agent ultimately resolves a request correctly. The outcome looks successful, but during the process it may have accessed information unnecessary for the task, repeatedly attempted a restricted action, relied on an untrusted external source, invoked an unusual combination of tools, or tried several unsuccessful strategies before landing on the right one. Outcome monitoring would call that interaction a success; behavioral monitoring might flag it as a warning. For autonomous systems, how an outcome was produced becomes part of the control environment.

3.3.3 What Should Institutions Observe?

Behavioral governance requires telemetry beyond conventional application logs — actions selected, tools invoked, resources accessed, permission requests, escalation frequency, failed actions, exceptions, decision sequences, proximity to financial or operational limits, interaction with other agents, policy violations, and shifts in the distribution of actions over time. The goal isn’t to record every internal computation indiscriminately; it’s to keep enough evidence to reconstruct consequential behavior and spot patterns that suggest the system is moving outside expected boundaries. That’s the real distinction between logging and observability — logging records events, observability helps an institution understand what those events collectively say about the system’s behavior.

3.3.4 Establish Behavioral Baselines

Detecting abnormal behavior requires some definition of normal behavior, which institutions can establish during testing and controlled deployment. A payments agent, for instance, might normally complete most routine cases without escalation, invoke a defined set of tools, rarely approach transaction limits, escalate unusual beneficiary changes, and never attempt certain restricted actions — production monitoring can then compare observed behavior against that profile.

The goal isn’t identical behavior every time; agentic systems are valuable precisely because they adapt to context. It’s identifying material deviations from approved behavioral boundaries — a sudden increase in tool usage, repeated attempts to access restricted resources, unusual delegation patterns, or growing concentration near control limits can warrant investigation even when business outcomes still look acceptable.

3.3.5 Individually Safe Actions Can Create Unsafe Trajectories

A particularly important challenge in agentic systems is that every individual action can appear permissible while the combined trajectory becomes problematic. Imagine an agent individually authorized to retrieve customer data, summarize information, create a document, and send an internal communication — each action legitimate on its own. But a particular sequence could combine sensitive information from several sources into a document and send it to someone who shouldn’t receive the aggregated whole. No individual tool call necessarily violates its permission boundary; the risk emerges from the composition of actions over time.

That means runtime governance can’t rely exclusively on asking is this action allowed? For higher-risk workflows, it also needs to ask: given everything this agent has already done, is this next action still appropriate? That’s the difference between action-level control and trajectory-level control.

3.3.6 Behavioral Monitoring Should Trigger Intervention

Observability without response is incomplete governance — institutions need to define what happens when behavior moves outside expected boundaries. Responses can be graduated by severity: observe (increase telemetry or flag unusual behavior), warn (generate an alert for review), constrain (reduce available tools, transaction limits, or permissions), escalate (require human approval before further consequential actions), suspend (temporarily stop the agent), and terminate (revoke authority and end execution) — a closed loop of observe, detect, evaluate, intervene, and learn. The goal isn’t just identifying problems after an incident; it’s catching emerging risk early enough to alter the agent’s behavior or authority before real harm occurs.

3.3.7 Explainability Also Changes

Traditional explainability asks why a model produced a particular prediction — which variables drove a credit-risk score, for instance. Agentic systems add a different question: why did the system take this sequence of actions? For consequential workflows, institutions may need to reconstruct the objective being pursued, the information available at the time, the actions selected, the tools used, the approvals obtained, the policy checks performed, and the resulting outcome — a form of decision provenance.

The goal isn’t necessarily to expose every internal reasoning token a foundation model generates; those traces can be unreliable, sensitive, or impractical to retain. What matters more is an auditable record of the observable decision process: context, actions, permissions, policy evaluations, tool interactions, and outcomes.

3.3.8 Monitoring Must Be Proportionate to Risk

Not every agent needs the same degree of surveillance — an internal agent summarizing meeting notes presents a very different risk profile than one capable of initiating payments or changing customer account information. Behavioral governance should be risk-based: higher autonomy and consequence should generally mean stronger telemetry, behavioral baselines, runtime policy checks, anomaly detection, human escalation, auditability, and intervention mechanisms. The greater the autonomy and potential consequence, the stronger the requirement for runtime observability and control.

3.3.9 From Model Monitoring to Behavioral Assurance

Traditional monitoring remains necessary, but agentic AI expands what institutions need to track — from asking whether the model is still performing, to whether business and customer outcomes are acceptable, to whether the agent is still acting as intended, to whether the institution can detect and intervene when it isn’t. Together, these form behavioral assurance: confidence not just that an AI system remains technically effective, but that its ongoing actions stay consistent with its approved purpose, authority, and risk boundaries.

Even a well-governed agent doesn’t operate alone. Its behavior depends on data, models, tools, APIs, memory, and increasingly other agents — and each component can look safe individually while their interactions create risks no component-level control would catch. That’s the next governance layer: Composition Governance, where safe components create unsafe systems.

3.4 Composition Governance — When Safe Components Create Unsafe Systems

Enterprise AI systems are rarely built from a single model. A production agent may combine a foundation model, enterprise data, retrieval, memory, business rules, tools, APIs, other agents, and core systems into a single chain, and every component along that chain can be individually tested, approved, and operating exactly as designed — while the overall system still produces an unacceptable outcome. That’s the fourth governance question: are the interactions between components as safe as the components themselves? This is composition risk.

3.4.1 Component Validation Does Not Equal System Validation

Traditional governance typically evaluates components individually — a model is validated, a data source is certified, an API is tested, an application passes security review, a vendor completes third-party risk assessment — and these controls remain necessary. But agentic systems introduce dependencies across components that may not be visible when each is evaluated separately.

Consider a commercial lending agent. The foundation model correctly interprets a request for a pricing exception. The customer data is accurate. The pricing engine correctly calculates available rates. The agent has legitimate access to the pricing API. The workflow system correctly executes the request it receives. Every component works as designed — yet the complete workflow could still produce an inappropriate pricing exception if the agent combines information incorrectly, fails to apply a required policy, or exercises authority in a context that authority was never intended for. The failure doesn’t belong neatly to the model, the data, the API, or the application; it emerges from their interaction.

3.4.2 Agentic AI Expands the Dependency Chain

Composition risk gets harder as agents gain access to more tools. A traditional predictive model has a relatively simple dependency chain — data, model, score, human decision — while an agentic workflow can look more like a request moving through an agent, retrieval, customer data, a policy engine, a specialized agent, an external tool, a decision, an approval, and a core system. Each additional interaction creates another place where context can be lost, permissions can change, data can cross boundaries, instructions can be misunderstood, untrusted information can enter the workflow, or one component can behave differently than another expects. Governance needs visibility into interfaces and dependencies, not just individual assets.

3.4.3 Permissions Must Survive Composition

Authority governance gets harder still when agents interact. Suppose an employee authorizes Agent A to analyze a customer relationship, Agent A delegates document analysis to Agent B, and Agent B calls a third-party service — the original authorization shouldn’t automatically become unrestricted authority throughout that chain. The relevant questions are what authority the employee originally delegated, which portion of that authority Agent A can pass along, what permissions Agent B should actually receive, whether the third-party service can reach the underlying customer data, whether authority expires when the task completes, and whether any downstream component can reuse the information for another purpose. This is permission continuity: authorization boundaries need to stay intact as tasks move across systems and agents. Without it, multi-agent architectures can unintentionally create privilege expansion.

3.4.4 Trusted Components Can Process Untrusted Information

Composition also opens new pathways for attacks. An agent, its tools, and the enterprise systems it accesses may all be trusted — but the content entering the workflow may not be. An agent reviewing customer documents could encounter malicious instructions embedded in a document or retrieved web content, and if it interprets those instructions as part of its task, untrusted content can end up influencing actions performed through trusted enterprise permissions. This is the fundamental danger behind indirect prompt injection: the problem isn’t simply that the model receives malicious text, it’s the combination of untrusted content, a trusted agent, and a privileged tool producing a potentially consequential action. Composition governance therefore requires a clear separation between information the agent may interpret and instructions the agent is authorized to follow.

3.4.5 Safe Actions Can Combine Into Unsafe Outcomes

Composition risk also shows up across sequences of otherwise legitimate actions. Imagine a wealth-management agent permitted to retrieve a client’s holdings, analyze portfolio concentration, generate recommendations, prepare an order, and send the proposed transaction for approval — each capability appropriate on its own. But suppose the agent retrieves outdated suitability information, combines it with current holdings, generates a recommendation, and passes a technically valid order into the approval workflow. No individual component necessarily failed; the problem is that the state of the complete system was inconsistent. This is why system-level validation needs to test not just whether components function, but whether the complete workflow behaves safely under stale or missing data, conflicting information, unavailable services, permission changes, unusual sequences, adversarial inputs, and partial failures.

3.4.6 Multi-Agent Systems Increase the Challenge

Composition gets more complex still when agents collaborate — a future financial workflow might chain a customer agent, credit agent, fraud agent, pricing agent, compliance agent, and execution agent together. Specialization can improve performance and control, since each agent has a narrower role, but it also creates coordination risk: agents may interpret objectives differently, operate with different information, make inconsistent assumptions, delegate tasks incorrectly, create feedback loops, or collectively produce an outcome none of them was individually designed to create. Multi-agent governance can’t be reduced to governing each agent independently — the interactions themselves need to be tested and monitored.

3.4.7 Third-Party Risk Becomes System Risk

Financial institutions already run mature third-party risk programs, and agentic AI complicates them. A single AI workflow may depend on a foundation-model provider, cloud infrastructure, vector databases, external datasets, identity services, agent frameworks, APIs, and specialized AI services, and a change by any one provider can alter the behavior of the complete system: a foundation-model upgrade may improve general capability while changing tool-selection behavior, an API modification may alter what information an agent receives, a retrieval service may change ranking behavior, a vendor policy change may affect data retention. Traditional vendor assessment asks whether a vendor is acceptable; agentic governance has to additionally ask what happens to the AI system when that vendor’s component changes. Third-party risk becomes increasingly inseparable from AI system risk.

3.4.8 Validate the Workflow, Not Just the Components

Composition governance requires testing at the level where business outcomes actually occur — the end-to-end workflow, not just its parts. Beyond normal operations, testing needs to cover interaction failures directly: what happens when a tool is unavailable, when two sources disagree, when an agent receives malicious retrieved content, when authorization changes mid-workflow, when one agent returns an unexpected response, when an external model changes behavior, or when several individually valid actions combine into a prohibited outcome. These aren’t conventional model-validation questions — they’re system-assurance questions.

3.4.9 Map Dependencies and Control Boundaries

A mature governance architecture needs an inventory not just of AI models and agents, but of the dependencies that make each agentic workflow possible — for consequential systems, institutions should be able to trace the full chain from agent through model, data, memory, tools, APIs, other agents, business systems, and external providers, and establish ownership, data boundaries, permission boundaries, trust boundaries, failure modes, monitoring responsibilities, and escalation paths along it. Without that visibility, organizations may know which AI assets they own without understanding the systems those assets collectively create.

3.4.10 From Component Assurance to System Assurance

Traditional controls remain essential — model validation establishes confidence in the model, data governance in the data, cybersecurity in systems and access, third-party risk management in external providers — but agentic AI adds a layer connecting them: system assurance, confidence that these components stay safe when they operate together. That’s the essence of composition governance, and it expands the governance boundary once more, from component risk to interaction risk to workflow risk to system risk.

For a single institution, this answers whether its own agentic system is safe within its own environment. Financial services introduces one further challenge: thousands of individually well-governed systems may consume similar information, use similar models, pursue similar objectives, and react to the same events. At that point the question is no longer whether one institution’s AI is safe — it’s what happens when autonomous systems across the financial system begin making similar decisions at the same time. That’s the final layer of the framework: Systemic Governance, where autonomous decisions become correlated.

3.5 Systemic Governance — When Autonomous Decisions Become Correlated

The first four layers of governance focus primarily on risks within an institution — objectives, authority, behavior, and interactions between components. Financial services introduces another dimension: an AI system can be well governed within one institution and still contribute to risk when many organizations deploy systems that respond to the same information in similar ways. That’s the fifth governance question: what happens when individually rational AI systems begin making similar decisions at the same time? This is systemic governance.

3.5.1 From Individual Risk to Collective Behavior

Financial institutions already manage concentration risk, liquidity risk, counterparty risk, and other exposures that can become significant when behavior is correlated. Agentic AI introduces another potential source: different institutions may increasingly rely on the same foundation models, similar market and economic data, common external data providers, comparable optimization techniques, similar risk signals, and overlapping technology vendors. The agents don’t need to communicate with each other to behave similarly — if they observe similar conditions and pursue similar objectives using similar models, they can independently arrive at similar decisions. That’s emergent correlation.

3.5.2 A Simple Market Example

Consider multiple investment firms deploying autonomous portfolio-management agents, each independently governed, each operating within approved risk limits, each responding appropriately to changing market conditions. Then volatility increases sharply, and if many agents interpret the same signals similarly, they may simultaneously reduce exposure. For each institution, selling may be rational; collectively, those decisions can amplify each other — lower prices raise volatility, which triggers more risk signals, which triggers more selling. The individual decisions remain defensible while the aggregate behavior becomes destabilizing.

This isn’t a new phenomenon; algorithmic trading and automated risk management have long raised concerns about feedback loops and crowded behavior. Agentic AI could expand the issue because increasingly capable systems can interpret broader information, formulate strategies, and act across multiple steps with less direct human intervention.

3.5.3 Evidence Should Be Interpreted Carefully

Recent research illustrates the potential risk but shouldn’t be mistaken for evidence that agentic AI is already destabilizing financial markets. Aldridge and Krawciw, for example, simulate correlated AI-driven strategies and show that increasing behavioral correlation can materially increase the probability of joint losses — but their results are synthetic scenarios, not observed market outcomes. That distinction matters: the argument here isn’t that agentic AI is creating systemic financial instability, it’s that as autonomous systems become more prevalent, correlated machine behavior is a risk worth measuring before it becomes material. That’s a more defensible basis for governance.

3.5.4 Systemic Risk Can Exist Outside Trading

The issue extends beyond capital markets. In payments, multiple institutions could deploy agents that dynamically respond to fraud indicators, and a common threat signal could cause many systems to tighten controls simultaneously, potentially disrupting legitimate transactions. In credit, institutions using similar data and AI models could react to deteriorating economic conditions by tightening underwriting at roughly the same time, reinforcing a credit contraction. In liquidity management, autonomous treasury systems responding to similar market signals could take correlated actions. In cybersecurity, agents responding automatically to perceived threats could isolate systems, block counterparties, or restrict activity in ways that propagate across interconnected financial infrastructure. None of these are predictions — they illustrate why the collective behavior of autonomous systems may eventually matter as much as the safety of any individual system.

3.5.5 Shared Models Create Concentration Risk

Systemic governance also intersects with third-party concentration. A large number of financial institutions may rely on a relatively small number of foundation-model providers, cloud platforms, AI infrastructure providers, data services, agent frameworks, and specialized vendors, which creates two different kinds of concentration: operational (failure at a widely used provider affects many institutions at once) and behavioral (systems built on similar models and information respond to events in similar ways). Traditional third-party risk management focuses heavily on the first; agentic AI may require much more attention to the second. The relevant question isn’t just how dependent an institution is on a given provider, but how similar its automated decisions might be to those of other institutions using the same underlying capabilities.

3.5.6 What Can an Individual Institution Do?

No single bank can govern the financial system, but institutions can still build systemic considerations into their own AI governance. For higher-impact autonomous systems, that means asking whether the institution is heavily dependent on a common foundation model or data provider, whether its agents could react procyclically during stress, what happens if external signals change abruptly, whether automated actions are subject to limits during abnormal conditions, whether autonomy can be reduced during periods of market or operational stress, whether circuit breakers exist for unusually rapid or concentrated activity, and whether the institution has tested scenarios involving correlated external behavior. Stress testing is particularly useful here — rather than testing only whether an agent performs under normal conditions, institutions can simulate environments where many market participants react similarly or where critical external services become constrained simultaneously.

3.5.7 The Role of Regulators

Some systemic risks can’t be observed from inside a single institution — a bank may understand its own agent’s behavior but have little visibility into whether hundreds of other institutions are deploying similar systems. That creates a role for regulators and industry bodies. Central banks and financial regulators are already beginning to examine AI through the lens of operational resilience and financial stability; the Bank of England, for example, has raised questions about how increasingly autonomous AI could affect markets, payments, cybersecurity, and the broader financial system. Over time, systemic AI governance could require industry-wide monitoring, AI-related stress testing, concentration analysis, incident information sharing, common resilience standards, and coordinated mechanisms for managing extreme events — extending AI governance beyond any single institution.

3.5.8 Governance at Multiple Levels

Systemic risk illustrates why agentic AI governance ultimately has to operate at several levels at once: whether the individual agent is behaving appropriately, whether its interactions with tools, data, and other agents are safe, whether aggregate AI activity is consistent with the firm’s risk appetite, and whether similar autonomous behavior across institutions could create concentration, feedback loops, or instability. The higher the level, the less control any individual institution has — but the need for visibility doesn’t disappear with it.

3.5.9 From Autonomous Decisions to Collective Resilience

Responsible AI has traditionally focused on the consequences of individual AI decisions — fairness, explainability, privacy, accountability, safety — and those principles remain essential. Agentic AI adds another question: what happens when autonomous decisions interact at scale? For financial institutions, responsible deployment can’t end with demonstrating that an individual model or agent is safe; as autonomy expands, governance will increasingly need to account for collective behavior, shared dependencies, concentration, and resilience under stress.

That completes the five dimensions of the framework — Objective, Authority, Behavior, Composition, Systemic. The next challenge is turning them into something institutions can actually implement: moving from a conceptual framework to an operational governance architecture that connects policy, technology, risk management, human oversight, and runtime controls.

4 A Governance Architecture for Agentic AI

The five governance dimensions — objective, authority, behavior, composition, and systemic risk — define what institutions need to govern. The next question is how to make that governance operational. Traditional governance is implemented primarily through policies, committees, documentation, validation, and periodic review, and those mechanisms remain essential — but autonomous systems create decisions continuously and at machine speed, so governance needs to exist not only around the AI system, but increasingly within its execution path.

A practical architecture can be organized around six interconnected capabilities: policy, identity and authority, runtime controls, observability, intervention, and audit and learning. Together, they translate Responsible AI principles into controls that operate before, during, and after an agent acts.

1. Policy — Define the Boundaries

Governance begins with institutional policy. Before an agent is deployed, the organization should establish its approved purpose, business owner, intended users, permitted objectives, prohibited outcomes, risk classification, data boundaries, level of autonomy, and human-oversight requirements — along with the institution’s tolerance for autonomous action generally. An internal research agent, for example, may have broad freedom to search approved information sources while having no authority to touch production systems; a payments agent operates under much tighter constraints because its actions can directly move funds. The goal is to convert broad Responsible AI principles into specific operating boundaries for each agentic use case.

2. Identity and Authority — Establish Who Can Act

Every production agent should have an identifiable, governable identity connecting it to an accountable business owner, an approved purpose, permitted resources, authorized tools, transaction or operational limits, and the human or process on whose behalf it acts. Authority should follow least privilege — an agent receives only the permissions its assigned task requires, and delegated authority stays bounded as work passes between agents — creating a traceable chain from owner to agent identity to delegated authority to resource to action. For consequential actions, authorization may also need to be reevaluated at execution time, so that changes in limits, approvals, customer status, or system state are reflected before the action actually occurs.

3. Runtime Controls — Enforce Governance During Execution

Policies become materially stronger when they can be enforced technically. Runtime controls sit between an agent’s decision and its ability to execute consequential actions — transaction limits, data-access restrictions, tool restrictions, eligibility rules, confidence thresholds, prohibited actions, segregation-of-duties checks, human-approval requirements, and contextual authorization, depending on the use case.

Consider an agent recommending and executing a customer pricing adjustment. The model may determine that a 40-basis-point concession is optimal, but before execution, a runtime policy layer verifies whether the customer is eligible, whether the agent has pricing authority, whether 40 basis points falls within its delegated limit, whether the action requires human approval, and whether anything has changed since the recommendation was generated. Only once those conditions are satisfied does the downstream system execute the action — an architectural separation where the AI decides what it wants to do, and governance determines whether it’s allowed to do it.

4. Observability — Understand What the Agent Is Doing

Runtime controls can’t anticipate every possible behavior, so institutions also need enough observability to understand how agents operate in production. For higher-risk systems, relevant telemetry includes actions taken, tools invoked, data sources accessed, authorization decisions, delegations, exceptions, failed attempts, human escalations, proximity to control limits, and resulting business outcomes. The goal isn’t simply accumulating logs — it’s being able to reconstruct what the agent was trying to accomplish, what information it used, what actions it took, which controls were evaluated, who authorized the activity, and what happened as a result. That reconstruction is the foundation for both behavioral monitoring and accountability.

5. Intervention — Maintain the Ability to Regain Control

Autonomy without intervention mechanisms creates unnecessary risk. When monitoring identifies unusual behavior or a policy violation, the institution needs to be able to respond proportionately: alert a human reviewer, escalate by requiring approval for subsequent actions, constrain by reducing limits or permissions or available tools, isolate by preventing interaction with specific systems or agents, suspend by temporarily stopping autonomous activity, or terminate by revoking authority and ending execution. These function as operational circuit breakers, and their importance grows with the speed, scale, and irreversibility of the actions an agent can take.

6. Audit and Learning — Close the Governance Loop

Governance shouldn’t end when an action completes — agent activity creates evidence that can improve the control environment. Institutions should periodically analyze policy violations, overrides, human interventions, anomalous behavior, customer outcomes, near misses, control failures, and incidents, and feed those observations back into objective design, permission boundaries, policies, testing scenarios, monitoring thresholds, human-oversight rules, and agent design itself. The architecture becomes a continuous loop — define, authorize, execute, observe, intervene, learn, refine — fundamentally different from treating AI governance as a one-time approval.

4.1 Governance Across the AI Lifecycle

These six capabilities operate across the lifecycle, not just at deployment. Before deployment, institutions define objectives, classify risk, establish ownership, validate components, test workflows, define authority, and set behavioral expectations. During execution, identity, authorization, policy enforcement, observability, human escalation, and intervention mechanisms govern the system in real time. After execution, audit, outcome monitoring, behavioral analysis, incident review, and control refinement provide ongoing assurance — design-time governance asking whether the institution is building the right system with appropriate boundaries, runtime governance asking whether the system is acting within those boundaries now, and post-execution governance asking whether the institution can demonstrate what happened and improve the controls. All three matter for higher-risk autonomous systems.

4.2 Connecting the Architecture to the Five Governance Dimensions

The architecture provides the control mechanisms for the five dimensions introduced earlier, and the framework isn’t a collection of independent controls — each layer reinforces the others.

Governance Dimension Primary Control Question Key Capabilities
Objective What should the agent pursue? Policy, constraints, outcome monitoring
Authority What can the agent do? Identity, permissions, limits, approvals
Behavior Is it still acting as intended? Observability, behavioral monitoring, intervention
Composition Are interactions safe? Dependency mapping, end-to-end testing, runtime policy
Systemic What happens at scale? Stress testing, concentration monitoring, resilience controls

4.3 Governance as a Control Plane

One useful way to think about this architecture is as an AI governance control plane: the business application and agent perform the work, and the governance layer determines the conditions under which that work can occur.

Business Objective → AI Agent / Multi-Agent System → Governance Control Plane (Identity | Authorization | Policy | Limits | Human Approval) → Tools / APIs / Enterprise Systems

Running alongside execution, a parallel assurance layer provides telemetry, behavioral monitoring, intervention, and audit. This separation matters — it lets institutions improve models or replace individual AI components without making the model itself solely responsible for enforcing every institutional policy. Governance becomes part of the enterprise architecture, not just a characteristic of the model.

4.4 From Responsible AI Principles to Executable Governance

Responsible AI principles remain the foundation, but agentic systems require translating them into operational mechanisms: accountability becomes identifiable ownership and agent identity; human oversight becomes risk-based approval and escalation; safety becomes limits, runtime policies, and circuit breakers; transparency becomes decision provenance and auditability; fairness becomes measurable outcome constraints and ongoing monitoring; security becomes least privilege, contextual authorization, and controlled tool access; reliability becomes behavioral monitoring, system testing, and resilience.

The critical transition, in short, is principle to policy to control to evidence. A governance principle without an enforceable control provides direction but limited protection; a control without evidence can’t demonstrate that governance actually worked. Agentic AI requires both.

The goal isn’t to eliminate autonomy — doing so would eliminate much of the value these systems create. It’s to make autonomy bounded, observable, interruptible, and accountable.

With the architecture established, the next challenge is organizational rather than technical: how should a financial institution actually govern, own, and oversee these systems? That’s where agentic AI governance moves from architecture into the enterprise operating model.

5 What This Means for Financial Institutions

The governance architecture described above has implications beyond AI technology. Agentic AI crosses organizational boundaries that financial institutions have traditionally managed through separate control functions — a single production agent may involve business processes, models, enterprise data, identity and access management, cybersecurity, third-party services, compliance requirements, and operational risk, and no single existing function owns all of these domains. The challenge isn’t to create another AI policy or governance committee; it’s to build an operating model that connects existing control disciplines around the complete AI system.

5.1 Start With the Use Case, Not the Model

The appropriate level of governance depends on what an AI system can actually do and the consequences if it fails. Consider four applications: an internal agent summarizing research, an employee-facing agent preparing customer communications, an agent recommending credit actions, and an agent capable of initiating payments. All may use the same underlying foundation model, but their risk profiles are fundamentally different — classifying systems primarily by model type provides limited information about the controls they actually need.

A more useful approach assesses the use case across dimensions like decision consequence, level of autonomy, data sensitivity, customer impact, financial authority, reversibility, regulatory significance, and external connectivity. That produces a governance principle financial institutions already know well: controls should be proportionate to risk.

5.2 Autonomy and Consequence Should Drive Control Intensity

Two dimensions matter most: autonomy and consequence. An agent with little autonomy and limited potential impact can run on relatively lightweight controls. One with substantial autonomy but low consequence needs stronger behavioral monitoring but fewer approval requirements. One making consequential recommendations without execution authority needs strong validation and human oversight. The highest level of governance applies where high autonomy meets high consequence:

Lower Consequence Higher Consequence
Lower Autonomy Standard controls Strong validation and human review
Higher Autonomy Runtime monitoring Maximum governance and runtime control

A payments agent capable of moving funds shouldn’t be governed the same way as an internal knowledge assistant, even if both use the same model — which gives institutions a practical basis for tiering agentic systems.

5.3 Business Ownership Remains Essential

Every agentic system needs an accountable business owner. Technology may build the system, data teams may supply information, Model Risk may validate components, cybersecurity may set technical controls — but the business has to stay accountable for the agent’s purpose, its expected outcome, acceptable operating boundaries, customer impact, and whether the system keeps creating appropriate business value. This matters most when an agent crosses several processes at once; without clear ownership, responsibility fragments across the same components that make agentic systems hard to govern in the first place.

5.4 Model Risk Management Still Has an Important Role

Agentic governance shouldn’t bypass Model Risk Management. Where an agent relies on models meeting the institution’s definition of a model, established practices — conceptual soundness, independent validation, data quality, performance monitoring, documentation, effective challenge — remain valuable. But MRM is one component of a broader architecture: it can answer whether a model is conceptually sound, performs appropriately, and has well-understood limitations, but it can’t independently answer whether an agent should have authority to execute a payment, whether it can delegate that authority, whether its tool usage is anomalous, whether an external document could manipulate its behavior, or what happens when several agents interact. Those questions need other control disciplines — which is particularly relevant given that the 2026 U.S. interagency model-risk guidance explicitly excludes generative and agentic AI from its scope while emphasizing the continued importance of broader risk-management practices for these systems.

5.5 Cybersecurity and Identity Become Core AI Governance Functions

As AI moves from generating information to taking actions, cybersecurity becomes inseparable from AI governance. Security teams need to address agent identity, credential management, least privilege, tool permissions, secrets management, external connectivity, prompt injection, data exfiltration, runtime monitoring, and incident response. Identity teams become particularly important here, because autonomous systems need attributable identities and controlled authority — IAM stops being infrastructure that merely supports AI and becomes part of the AI control environment itself.

5.6 Data Governance Expands From Data Quality to Context

Data governance grows more important too. Traditional concerns — accuracy, completeness, lineage, privacy, retention, access — remain, but agentic systems add context: an agent may combine information from multiple sources, retrieve documents dynamically, maintain memory across interactions, or act on information that’s changed since it was retrieved. Institutions need to know which data informed a decision, when it was retrieved, whether it was authoritative, whether the agent was permitted to use it, and whether the relevant context was still valid when the action occurred. Data lineage increasingly becomes part of decision provenance.

5.7 Compliance Must Translate Requirements Into Operational Boundaries

Compliance functions traditionally interpret regulations and set policy. Agentic systems create both an opportunity and a requirement to move some of that closer to execution — a compliance requirement can translate into prohibited actions, eligibility conditions, communication restrictions, mandatory disclosures, escalation thresholds, or approval requirements. Not every regulatory judgment can or should be encoded as software, but where requirements are clear and deterministic, converting them into machine-enforceable policy reduces reliance on an agent correctly interpreting broad compliance language on its own. Compliance becomes a participant in defining what the agent may and may not do, rather than only reviewing the system after it’s already been designed.

5.8 Operational Risk Connects the System

Operational Risk plays a particularly important role because many agentic failures won’t fit neatly into model risk or cybersecurity — an agent can behave exactly as designed and still create process failures, customer harm, financial loss, control breakdowns, concentration risk, or unexpected dependencies. Operational Risk connects these through risk assessments, control design, scenario analysis, incident management, resilience testing, and risk acceptance, and that role only grows as AI moves deeper into core business processes.

5.9 Third-Party Risk Must Evaluate Dependencies, Not Just Vendors

Agentic AI can create long dependency chains — a single workflow may depend on a foundation-model provider, cloud platform, external dataset, identity provider, agent framework, and several APIs. Traditional third-party assessment remains necessary, but institutions also need to understand how these dependencies interact: what happens if a provider becomes unavailable, what happens if a model gets upgraded, whether the institution can switch providers, which data leaves the institution, which provider changes could alter system behavior, and whether several critical agents depend on the same provider. This shifts third-party governance from evaluating individual vendors toward understanding concentration and dependency across the whole AI architecture.

5.10 Governance Should Not Become Another Silo

There’s a natural temptation to respond to all of this by creating a new, centralized “Agentic AI Risk” function — but that risks becoming another silo. A better model is federated governance with centralized standards: a central AI governance function sets enterprise policies, risk classification, minimum controls, architecture standards, documentation requirements, monitoring expectations, and escalation procedures, while existing control functions apply their own expertise within that frame — business for purpose and outcomes, data for quality and lineage and context, technology for architecture and reliability, cybersecurity and IAM for identity and permissions and security, Model Risk for validation and limitations, compliance and legal for regulatory boundaries, operational risk for process and resilience, internal audit for independent assurance. This preserves specialist accountability while creating one common framework.

5.11 Create an Enterprise Inventory of Agents

Institutions can’t govern systems they can’t see. As agents get easier to build, organizations risk creating the AI equivalent of shadow IT — agents developed across business units with inconsistent ownership, permissions, monitoring, and documentation. An enterprise inventory needs to capture more than an agent’s name: for each material agent, institutions should know its owner, purpose, underlying models, data accessed, tools available, level of autonomy, delegated permissions, downstream systems, third-party dependencies, risk classification, human-oversight requirements, and production status. That inventory becomes the foundation for governance, monitoring, concentration analysis, and eventually audit.

5.12 Govern Autonomy Progressively

Financial institutions don’t need to move immediately from copilots to fully autonomous systems — a more controlled approach expands autonomy as evidence accumulates. An AI system might progress from observing (analyzing information but taking no action) to recommending (proposing an action for human approval) to preparing (completing the workflow but requiring approval before execution) to executing within limits (independently handling defined low-risk actions) to expanded autonomy (handling broader workflows under continuous runtime controls). Each stage produces evidence about performance, behavior, control effectiveness, and operational impact before more authority is granted — autonomy becomes something the system earns through demonstrated control effectiveness, not a capability granted simply because the technology supports it.

5.13 Governance Should Enable Adoption, Not Prevent It

Strong governance is sometimes framed as being in tension with innovation. For financial institutions, the opposite is often true: without clear boundaries, organizations may hesitate to deploy higher-value AI because the risks are hard to quantify. A well-designed governance architecture removes that hesitation — business teams know what they can automate, technology teams know what controls to build, risk functions know what evidence to expect, executives understand where human accountability remains, and regulators can see how institutional principles translate into actual controls. The goal of agentic AI governance isn’t to minimize autonomy; it’s to create the conditions under which institutions can increase autonomy safely and deliberately.

The final question is how to begin. Financial institutions don’t need to implement the entire target-state architecture at once — a practical path can start with the agents already being developed today and progressively build the controls greater autonomy requires. That’s the focus of the next section: A Practical Path Forward.

6 A Practical Path Forward

Financial institutions don’t need to build a complete agentic AI governance architecture before experimenting with AI agents — but governance should evolve before autonomy expands, not after. A practical approach starts with visibility, establishes common risk boundaries, implements controls around the highest-risk actions, and progressively increases autonomy as the institution builds evidence that those controls work. This can be organized into six steps.

Step 1 — Inventory Agentic AI Use Cases

The first priority is visibility. Institutions should identify agentic systems in production, being piloted, under development, or provided through third-party platforms, and the inventory should capture more than the underlying model: for each material use case, understand the business purpose, accountable owner, underlying models, data accessed, available tools, external connectivity, downstream systems, third-party dependencies, level of autonomy, and actions the agent can execute. This creates the baseline for every subsequent governance decision — the objective is simply to know where agents exist and what they can do.

Step 2 — Classify by Autonomy and Consequence

Not every agent needs the same governance. Institutions should classify use cases by level of autonomy, financial consequence, customer impact, regulatory significance, data sensitivity, reversibility of actions, and external connectivity — a knowledge assistant summarizing internal policies shouldn’t face the same control requirements as an agent capable of changing customer information or initiating a payment. The intersection of autonomy and consequence is a useful starting point: higher autonomy combined with higher consequence should trigger stronger requirements for validation, authorization, observability, human oversight, and runtime intervention.

Step 3 — Establish the Governance Contract

Before a material agent receives production authority, the institution should explicitly define its operating boundaries — think of it as a governance contract for the agent, answering what it’s expected to accomplish, what outcomes it’s optimizing, what data and tools and systems and actions it can access, what’s explicitly prohibited, when it must escalate to a human, what behavior and outcomes will be observed, under what conditions its authority will be reduced or suspended, and who owns it and accepts responsibility for its operation. This converts broad governance principles into explicit operating expectations.

Step 4 — Put Controls in the Execution Path

The most important controls shouldn’t depend solely on the agent remembering or interpreting policy correctly — where practical, they should be enforced outside the model, through authentication and agent identity, least-privilege access, transaction limits, approved tool lists, data-access restrictions, segregation-of-duties rules, contextual authorization, human-approval thresholds, and circuit breakers. This separates the intelligence generating an action from the governance determining whether that action can occur: don’t rely on the model to enforce controls the surrounding system can enforce deterministically. The agent may recommend an action; the control layer determines whether it’s permitted.

Step 5 — Test the System, Not Just the Model

Before increasing autonomy, institutions should test the complete workflow under conditions that reflect real operating risk — expected scenarios, but also missing or stale data, conflicting information, unavailable tools, permission changes, malicious retrieved content, unexpected agent responses, third-party failures, repeated actions, delegation between agents, and attempts to exceed control limits. The goal is to answer how the system fails, and just as important, whether the controls fail safely when it does. For higher-risk systems, scenario testing and adversarial testing should become part of the approval process itself.

Step 6 — Expand Autonomy Progressively

Autonomy should increase as evidence accumulates — an institution might move from observing, to recommending, to preparing, to executing within limits, to expanded autonomy. At each stage, governance teams can evaluate model performance, business outcomes, behavioral stability, policy violations, human overrides, control effectiveness, operational incidents, and customer impact, and only grant additional authority once the institution has sufficient confidence. That makes autonomy earned rather than assumed.

6.1 Measure Governance Effectiveness

Institutions should also measure whether the governance framework itself is working — useful indicators include the percentage of agents with identified owners, the percentage classified by risk, the percentage operating under unique identities, the frequency of policy violations, human escalation and override rates, anomalous tool usage, attempted unauthorized actions, control-trigger frequency, time to detect abnormal behavior, time to revoke agent authority, and AI-related incidents or near misses. These measures shift the question governance asks from do we have an AI policy to can we demonstrate our controls are working.

6.2 Build Toward Continuous Assurance

The long-term goal shouldn’t be continuous human review of every AI decision — that would defeat much of the value autonomous systems create. Instead, institutions should move toward continuous assurance: policies define boundaries, technology enforces critical controls, telemetry provides visibility, monitoring detects abnormal behavior, humans intervene when judgment is required, and audit provides independent assurance. That’s a governance model that can scale as the number and autonomy of AI agents increase.

6.3 Start With Control, Then Earn Autonomy

The transition to agentic AI doesn’t require financial institutions to choose between innovation and governance — it requires sequencing the two correctly: start with limited authority, observe how the system behaves, build evidence, strengthen controls, then expand autonomy. The institutions best positioned to capture value from agentic AI may not be those that automate fastest. They may be the ones that earn the confidence to automate further, because they can demonstrate that autonomy remains bounded, observable, and accountable.

7 Conclusion — From Principles to Executable Governance

Financial institutions have spent decades developing sophisticated frameworks for governing models, technology, data, security, and operational risk, and those disciplines remain essential as AI becomes more capable. Agentic AI doesn’t invalidate them — it changes the unit of governance. When AI systems can reason across multiple steps, use tools, interact with other systems, delegate tasks, and take actions, governing the underlying model is no longer enough; institutions increasingly need to govern the complete decision system in which that model operates.

That requires answering five questions: what is the agent trying to achieve (Objective); what is it permitted to do (Authority); is it still acting as intended (Behavior); are its interactions with data, tools, models, and other agents safe (Composition); and what happens when autonomous decisions interact at scale (Systemic). Together, these dimensions extend Responsible AI beyond traditional model governance.

Identifying the risks, though, is only the first step. The larger challenge is translating governance principles into controls that operate where autonomous decisions actually happen: accountability needs identifiable ownership, human oversight needs defined escalation and approval boundaries, security needs least privilege and controlled tool access, transparency needs decision provenance and auditability, safety needs enforceable limits and intervention mechanisms, and fairness needs measurable outcomes and ongoing monitoring. Responsible AI principles, in short, need an executable control layer.

That doesn’t mean every governance requirement should become software — many decisions will continue to require judgment, independent challenge, policy interpretation, and human accountability — and it doesn’t mean every AI application should face the same controls. Governance should stay proportionate to a system’s autonomy, potential consequence, and operating environment; a low-risk internal assistant and an autonomous payments agent shouldn’t be governed identically. The objective is bounded autonomy: letting AI systems operate independently where appropriate, while keeping clear limits on what they can access, decide, and execute.

For financial institutions, this may ultimately be the most important shift. The competitive advantage from agentic AI won’t come simply from deploying more agents or granting them greater autonomy — as the technology becomes widely available, those capabilities will increasingly become commodities. The more durable advantage will come from an institution’s ability to trust autonomous systems with consequential work, and that trust can’t rest on model capability alone. It has to be earned through governance that makes autonomous behavior bounded, observable, interruptible, and accountable.

The evolution of Responsible AI, then, isn’t from governance to autonomy — it’s from governing models to governing autonomous decision-making systems, and from principles documented in policy to controls demonstrated in operation.
