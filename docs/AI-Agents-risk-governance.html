Rethinking the Three Lines for Agentic Banking

How autonomy changes business ownership, risk oversight, and assurance

Introduction

The first generation of AI governance in banking was built around models. Models were developed for defined purposes, independently validated, approved for use, monitored for performance, and periodically reviewed. As generative AI emerged, institutions expanded those frameworks to address new risks such as hallucination, bias, data leakage, and inappropriate use.

Agentic AI creates a more fundamental challenge. An AI agent can interpret an objective, retrieve information, invoke tools, interact with enterprise systems, make decisions, and execute actions across an end-to-end workflow. At sufficient scale, thousands of these decisions may occur before a traditional governance process has an opportunity to review even a sample of them.

This changes not only what banks need to govern, but also how governance itself must operate.

In my earlier article on Agentic AI Governance, I argued that governance needs to evolve from model governance toward system governance—and increasingly become part of the execution architecture itself.

That evolution can be viewed across three generations of enterprise AI:

Predictive AI Generative AI Agentic AI
Primary role Predict / score Generate / assist Decide / act
Human role Makes decision Reviews output Supervises boundaries & exceptions
Governance object Model Model + use case End-to-end system
Primary concern Model performance Output reliability & appropriate use Authority, behavior & execution
Governance approach Validate & monitor Guardrails & oversight Runtime / executable governance
Control timing Pre-deployment + periodic Pre/post output Before, during & after execution
Key question Is the model performing as intended? Can we trust the output? Is the system behaving as intended and authorized to act?

Exhibit 1: Evolution of AI Governance.

The progression is important. Predictive AI largely required institutions to govern models. Generative AI expanded the problem toward governing models and their use. Agentic AI expands it again toward governing systems that can take actions.

This creates a second-order question that receives less attention: What happens to the bank’s governance operating model when AI systems begin operating at machine speed?

The Three Lines remain important. Their underlying principles—business ownership, independent challenge, accountability, and assurance—do not disappear.

What changes is how each line exercises those responsibilities.

1. Governing AI at Machine Speed

Traditional banking governance assumes that humans remain central to the decision process. The first line operates the business and manages its risks. The second line establishes risk policies, provides independent challenge, and monitors adherence. The third line independently assesses whether the control environment is appropriately designed and operating effectively.

This structure works when consequential decisions occur at a speed and volume humans can reasonably supervise. Agentic AI changes that assumption.

Consider an autonomous credit-monitoring workflow that continuously analyzes borrowers, retrieves updated information, evaluates risk indicators, invokes models and tools, identifies exceptions, and initiates downstream actions.

Reviewing every decision would eliminate much of the value of automation. Sampling decisions after the fact is also insufficient. Individual decisions may remain within approved limits while, collectively, the agent’s behavior gradually pushes the institution beyond its intended risk boundaries.

The governance challenge therefore shifts from periodically reviewing an approved system to continuously ensuring that it operates within established objectives, authority, and boundaries. The Three Lines remain relevant, but they must evolve to govern systems that operate continuously.

2. First Line: Accountability for Agentic AI

The first line’s fundamental accountability does not change. The business remains responsible for the outcome and the risks created by the activity. But owning an agentic system requires more than sponsoring an AI use case. When an AI system can make decisions and take actions, the business must understand and own its delegated authority.

A first-line owner should be able to answer:

  • What objective has this agent been given?
  • What decisions can it make autonomously?
  • What data and systems can it access?
  • What actions can it execute?
  • When must it escalate to a human?
  • What happens when its behavior moves outside expected boundaries?

This changes the meaning of business ownership. For traditional analytics, ownership might mean approving requirements and accepting model outputs. For agentic AI, ownership increasingly means accepting accountability for a continuously operating decision system.

That accountability should be explicit. An agent should operate under delegated authority from an identifiable business owner, with defined objectives, permissions, limits, escalation requirements, and conditions under which that authority can be reduced or revoked.

For agentic AI, first-line ownership extends to the agent’s mandate, delegated authority, and operating boundaries.

3. Second Line: Governing the Boundaries of Autonomy

Traditional second-line oversight relies on policies, approvals, monitoring, independent challenge, and escalation. These mechanisms remain necessary, but the second line cannot review thousands—or eventually millions—of autonomous decisions. Its role increasingly centers on defining the boundaries within which autonomous systems can operate.

These boundaries might include:

  • permitted levels of autonomy;
  • transaction or exposure limits;
  • approval thresholds;
  • prohibited actions;
  • data-access restrictions;
  • escalation conditions.

Where appropriate, these requirements should be machine-enforceable. For example, if exposures above a certain threshold require human approval, the control architecture should prevent the agent from proceeding without it.

Not every risk policy can—or should—be converted into code. Some decisions will continue to require human judgment. The key is determining which boundaries can be enforced by the system and where human judgment is required.

The second line therefore defines and challenges the risk envelope within which autonomy can operate, without becoming the operator of the system.

4. Third Line: Assurance for Agentic Systems

Agentic AI changes the focus of independent assurance. Traditional audit approaches often sample transactions to determine whether required processes and controls were followed. Sampling will remain important, but it cannot provide sufficient assurance over systems making decisions continuously at machine scale.

Internal Audit will increasingly need to assess whether the governance and control architecture is appropriately designed and operating effectively. Key questions include:

  • Were approved authority boundaries enforced?
  • Were prohibited actions blocked?
  • Were required human approvals triggered?
  • Were permissions appropriately scoped?
  • Did monitoring identify material changes in behavior?
  • Can consequential decisions be reconstructed?

The broader question is whether the institution can demonstrate who—or what—acted, under whose authority, subject to which controls, and with what outcome.

The third line therefore provides assurance not only over individual decisions, but also over the governance system that continuously controls agent behavior.

5. Human Judgment at the Edge

Machine-speed governance does not mean removing humans from risk management. It requires being more deliberate about where human judgment adds value.

Requiring humans to approve thousands of routine AI decisions may create bottlenecks and encourage rubber-stamping rather than meaningful oversight. Human intervention should focus on situations involving ambiguity, significant consequences, or conditions outside established boundaries.

Examples might include:

  • unusually large exposures;
  • conflicting evidence;
  • material customer impact;
  • exceptions to established risk appetite;
  • situations the system has not previously encountered.

Routine decisions can proceed within established controls, while exceptions are directed to accountable human decision-makers.

This provides a more practical interpretation of human-in-the-loop: humans should be involved where judgment matters, not in every decision. As agents become more capable, defining these intervention points becomes an important part of governance.

6. The Architecture That Connects the Three Lines

The responsibilities of the Three Lines remain distinct, but agentic AI requires a technical governance architecture that connects them. In my earlier article, I described this as an AI Governance Control Plane:

The AI determines what it wants to do. Governance determines whether it is allowed to do it.

The control plane translates governance requirements into runtime controls. The first line owns the business mandate and outcome, the second line establishes risk boundaries, and the third line provides independent assurance. Technology makes these requirements executable.

Three capabilities are particularly important.

Machine-Readable Policy

Critical policies and risk boundaries should be machine-enforceable where appropriate. These might include permissions, transaction limits, prohibited actions, segregation-of-duties requirements, escalation thresholds, and human-approval requirements.

The objective is not to automate judgment, but to ensure that autonomous systems operate within established boundaries.

A Machine-Readable View of the Bank

Agents need reliable context about the entities and relationships on which policies operate. A customer, for example, may simultaneously be a borrower, guarantor, beneficial owner, counterparty, or business owner. A loan may connect to collateral, covenants, exposures, risk ratings, and regulatory classifications.

This creates an important role for semantic layers, ontologies, and knowledge graphs that provide governed meaning and relationships across enterprise data. These capabilities become part of the governance infrastructure because an agent cannot reliably apply policy without understanding the entities, relationships, and context to which that policy applies.

Continuous Evidence

Agentic systems also need to generate evidence as decisions occur. For consequential actions, institutions should be able to reconstruct what the agent did, under whose authority, which controls were applied, and what outcome resulted.

This is more than logging. It provides decision provenance—a traceable record of the authority, controls, actions, and outcomes associated with an agent’s decisions.

Continuous evidence makes governance demonstrable rather than merely documented.

7. An AI-Native Three Lines Model

The Three Lines remain relevant for agentic AI, but how each line exercises its responsibilities evolves.

Line Traditional emphasis Agentic AI emphasis
First Line Own processes, decisions, and operating risks Own agent mandate, delegated authority, outcomes, and operating boundaries
Second Line Establish policy, review risk, monitor and challenge Define the risk envelope, autonomy limits, executable boundaries, monitoring and escalation requirements
Third Line Test controls and sample transactions Independently assure the governance architecture and whether controls operate effectively at runtime

Exhibit 2: Evolution of the Three Lines for Agentic AI.

The Three Lines are not being replaced. Their responsibilities are being adapted to systems that can make and execute decisions continuously. Business ownership, independent risk oversight, and assurance remain distinct, while the governance architecture makes those responsibilities operational at machine speed.

Conclusion — Governance at Machine Speed

Agentic AI challenges a fundamental assumption of traditional governance: that oversight can operate primarily on human time. As autonomous systems make and execute decisions continuously, governance must be able to operate alongside them.

This does not diminish the importance of human judgment. It makes where humans intervene more important, concentrating their attention on decisions involving ambiguity, significant consequences, or exceptions to established boundaries.

The Three Lines remain relevant. Business ownership, independent risk oversight, and assurance are still essential, but the mechanisms supporting them must keep pace with increasingly autonomous systems.

When decisions operate at machine speed, governance must be capable of operating at machine speed as well.
